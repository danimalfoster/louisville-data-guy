# Louisville Data Guy voice guide

How posts and glossary entries on this site should sound. Built from Daniel's own writing (a
professional bio, an email to his youth baseball team's parents, and work emails explaining data
and proposing projects). Follow this for everything written for the site. Update it as Daniel edits.

## The short version

Write like Daniel explaining something to a colleague or stakeholder over email: direct, practical,
considerate, and honest about the messy parts. Professional subject matter, conversational delivery.
Somewhere between his formal bio and his coaching email, closer to the email.

## How Daniel writes

**Leads with the answer or recommendation, then explains.** "The simplest, most efficient way to
fulfill this request is to..." Then why, then what it takes, then how long. He doesn't build up to the
point.

**Gives opinions in first person, plainly.** "I recommend," "my suggestion would be," "it would make
sense to me," "I think it's also important to consider." Clear positions, stated politely, without
hype or absolutes.

**Explains the reason behind things.** "I don't have an assistant coach, so it would be a big help if..."
"Having a separate server will ensure that we don't cause any performance issues." The *so* and
*because* are always there.

**Sequences in prose, not formatting.** "First off," "The first step would be," "Once that's done,"
"After we've...," "From there," "That said," "Now, for the rest of the season." Structure comes from
transitions, not headings and bullets. Numbered lists only when the content really is a list of
criteria or steps.

**Uses everyday analogies.** "Think of this as taking the whole conversation and separating out just
the questions and answers."

**Admits uncertainty and messiness.** "Hopefully, the transcription software can identify who is
talking, otherwise this will get a little murky." "I don't know how much X has communicated with you,
so I figured I'd..." He doesn't pretend things are cleaner than they are.

**Thinks about constraints and the bigger picture.** Cost, scale, performance on production systems,
what infrastructure already exists, where a project fits in the long-term plan, who has to do the work.

**Precise about definitions, then gives the bottom line.** He explains exactly what two similar-looking
columns mean and why they differ, then: "If you are looking for the total number of calls an agent
handled, use Column H."

**Considerate of the reader.** "If you have time," "When you have a minute," "if no one has told you
yet." Meets people where they are without talking down.

**Ends plainly.** "See you all tomorrow." "That's all I've got for now." "Let me know if you have any
questions." No summary, no clever closing line.

**Word choice.** Contractions. Everyday words. Light business idioms are fine ("go down this road,"
"sunset," "big-picture," "churn through," "holistic"). Parenthetical asides "(not just orders)" are
natural to him. Medium-length sentences joined with *so*, *which*, *because*; not choppy fragments.

## What he cares about (the point of view)

- Trust between data teams and the business. He has repaired relationships and rebuilt confidence in
  data teams through frequent communication, managing expectations, iterative delivery, and delivering
  on time. This theme belongs in the writing wherever it fits.
- Practical over theoretical. What it takes, what it costs, what can go wrong, what to do Monday.
- Range from hands-on (importing, cleansing, validating, SQL) to architecture to leadership.
- Current work: connecting business users to data through AI and MCP, with guardrails.

## Avoid (Claude's habits, not Daniel's)

- The same template on every page. Glossary entries share a definition box; the body varies.
- Bold labels at the start of bullets ("**Accuracy:** ..."). Bold text in general, nearly always.
- Bolded myth quotes as a section ("**"X is always better."**").
- "It's not X, it's Y" / "X isn't about A. It's about B." constructions.
- Lists of exactly three, over and over.
- Neat aphoristic closers ("The second can be checked. The first cannot.").
- Em dashes. Use commas, parentheses, or a new sentence.
- Colon-heavy sentences and lots of headings.
- Generic hypotheticals ("A regional bank...") when a real, generalized experience of Daniel's fits.
- Filler like "In practice," "Crucially," "It's worth noting," "Here's the thing," "Let's dive in."

## Privacy

Use Daniel's real experiences only in generalized form. No employer-internal details, customer or
vendor names, colleague names, internal system names, or figures. Flag any anecdote drawn from his
work for his approval during review.

## Glossary entries

Keep the `short` definition (it powers the definition box, the A-Z page, and search snippets). In the
body: say where the reader is likely to run into the term, explain it plainly, ground it in a real
example (ideally Daniel's), give his recommendation, and note what to watch out for. Use headings only
when a long entry needs them, and phrase them naturally. Length should fit the term.
